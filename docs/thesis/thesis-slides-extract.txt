### ppt/slides/slide5.xml
1.1 Background The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), Internet of Things (IoT), and embedded computing technologies has accelerated the development of intelligent systems capable of perceiving, understanding, and interacting with the physical environment. Traditionally, AI applications have primarily focused on processing digital information through centralized computing infrastructures. However, emerging applications require intelligent systems to operate directly in dynamic physical environments, where sensing, learning, reasoning, and decision-making must be performed under real-time constraints. This evolution has contributed to the emergence of Physical Artificial Intelligence (Physical AI), which integrates AI algorithms with physical systems to enable autonomous perception, intelligent decision-making, and adaptive interaction with the surrounding environment. Physical AI has become an important technological foundation for various applications, including autonomous vehicles, intelligent robotics, smart manufacturing, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), and intelligent transportation systems. Unlike conventional AI applications, Physical AI systems simultaneously consider computational efficiency, communication reliability, environmental uncertainty, and physical operational constraints. As Physical AI applications become increasingly complex, the capabilities of individual intelligent devices are often insufficient to accomplish sophisticated tasks independently. A single autonomous robot, for example, may possess limited sensing coverage, computational resources, and environmental knowledge. Similarly, an autonomous vehicle may encounter situations that cannot be adequately understood using only its locally collected observations. These limitations motivate the development of Collaborative Physical AI, in which multiple intelligent devices cooperate by exchanging information, sharing learned knowledge, and coordinating their actions to achieve common objectives. Collaborative Physical AI extends the capabilities of individual devices by combining distributed sensing, computation, and intelligence across interconnected physical agents. Through collaboration, intelligent systems can improve environmental awareness, enhance decision-making, and adapt more effectively to changing operational conditions. However, establishing effective collaboration among geographically distributed devices introduces significant challenges related to communication overhead, heterogeneous hardware resources, data privacy, network dynamics, and distributed learning.

### ppt/slides/slide6.xml
1.1 Background The integration of edge computing with AI provides an important foundation for addressing the computational and communication requirements of Collaborative Physical AI networks. Edge computing enables data processing and intelligent inference to be performed closer to the data sources, reducing dependence on remote cloud infrastructures. In Physical AI environments, edge devices such as embedded computing platforms, industrial controllers, autonomous robots, and intelligent sensors can locally process sensory information and execute AI models. This distributed computing paradigm can reduce communication latency, improve responsiveness, and limit the transmission of sensitive raw data. However, edge devices typically exhibit substantial differences in computational capabilities, available memory, energy consumption, and communication bandwidth. Furthermore, the information collected by individual devices is frequently heterogeneous because devices operate in different physical locations and encounter distinct environmental conditions. Consequently, developing a collaborative learning mechanism that effectively utilizes distributed data and computational resources while accommodating device heterogeneity has become an important research direction. Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a promising distributed machine learning paradigm for enabling collaborative model training without requiring the direct exchange of raw training data. In conventional centralized machine learning, data collected from multiple sources are generally transferred to a central computing infrastructure for model training. Although this approach can leverage large-scale datasets, it introduces challenges associated with communication bandwidth, centralized data storage, privacy protection, and network dependence. In contrast, FL allows participating devices to train machine learning models using their locally available datasets and share model parameters or updates rather than raw data. These updates are subsequently aggregated to construct a shared model that incorporates knowledge obtained from multiple participants. The foundational Federated Averaging ( FedAvg ) approach demonstrated that iterative local training and model aggregation can support communication-efficient collaborative learning across distributed devices (McMahan et al., 2017). As a result, FL provides a suitable foundation for collaborative intelligence in environments where data are inherently distributed and transferring raw information to centralized infrastructures is impractical. However, keeping raw data local does not inherently guarantee complete privacy, because shared model updates may still reveal sensitive information under certain conditions.

### ppt/slides/slide7.xml
1.1 Background To overcome the limitations of server-dependent architectures, Decentralized Federated Learning (DFL) has attracted increasing research attention as an alternative distributed learning paradigm. Unlike conventional server-based FL, DFL enables participating nodes to collaboratively train machine learning models through direct peer-to-peer communication without relying on a central server for model aggregation. In this architecture, each node performs local model training using its private dataset and exchanges model information with neighboring nodes according to an established communication topology. Through iterative local training and decentralized aggregation, knowledge can propagate throughout the network, allowing participating nodes to develop collaborative models without requiring centralized parameter aggregation. This decentralized approach offers potential advantages in terms of scalability, fault tolerance, communication locality, and operational flexibility. Consequently, DFL is particularly relevant to Collaborative Physical AI networks, where intelligent devices must continuously adapt to distributed environments while maintaining a degree of operational autonomy.

### ppt/slides/slide8.xml
1.1 Background However, removing centralized model aggregation also introduces additional challenges related to communication coordination, network topology design, model convergence, and heterogeneous resource management. In DFL systems, model information is exchanged through a network of interconnected learning nodes, and the communication topology determines how knowledge propagates across the system. Dense communication topologies can facilitate rapid information dissemination but may result in excessive communication overhead and increased resource consumption. Conversely, sparse communication topologies can reduce communication costs but may slow knowledge propagation and negatively affect learning convergence. These trade-offs are further complicated by variations in network bandwidth, communication latency, node availability, and computational capabilities. Therefore, the performance of a DFL system depends not only on its learning algorithms but also on the underlying network structure and communication strategies. Recent research on peer-to-peer federated learning identifies dynamic topology adaptation, communication efficiency, heterogeneous devices, and realistic network conditions as important challenges in deploying decentralized learning systems. Beyond communication topology, another fundamental challenge in DFL systems is the heterogeneity of participating devices and their learning environments. Collaborative Physical AI networks may consist of various computing platforms, ranging from resource-constrained embedded devices to high-performance edge computing systems. These devices differ in processing speed, memory capacity, available energy, and communication capabilities, making the deployment of identical neural network architectures across all participants inefficient or impractical. Moreover, local training datasets are frequently characterized by non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data, resulting from variations in sensing conditions, geographical locations, device functionalities, and operational environments. Such data heterogeneity can cause inconsistencies in local model updates and adversely affect collaborative learning performance. Consequently, effective DFL systems require adaptive mechanisms capable of accommodating both computational heterogeneity and statistical heterogeneity while maintaining acceptable model accuracy and efficient resource utilization.

### ppt/slides/slide10.xml
1.1 Background Neural Architecture Search (NAS) provides a promising approach to addressing model adaptation challenges in heterogeneous distributed learning environments. NAS automates the process of identifying suitable neural network architectures according to predefined performance objectives and resource constraints. Rather than relying on manually designed neural networks, NAS explores architectural configurations involving network depth, layer width, kernel size, and other structural parameters to identify models that balance prediction accuracy and computational efficiency. In particular, the Once-for-All (OFA) approach introduced by Cai et al. (2020) demonstrated that a single supernetwork can support a large collection of specialized subnetworks suitable for different hardware platforms and resource constraints. By separating supernetwork training from architecture specialization, OFA provides an efficient mechanism for selecting deployment-specific neural network configurations. Integrating resource-aware NAS principles into DFL environments therefore offers opportunities to support heterogeneous learning nodes while reducing the computational burden associated with maintaining separate neural network architectures. Nevertheless, adapting NAS to decentralized collaborative training introduces additional considerations regarding model compatibility, distributed optimization, knowledge sharing, and communication efficiency. In addition to learning and communication optimization, the effective operation of DFL systems requires mechanisms for monitoring network conditions, evaluating system performance, and supporting adaptive management. Digital Twin (DT) technology has emerged as a promising paradigm for representing physical systems through synchronized virtual models. A digital twin maintains a digital representation of a physical entity or system using continuously or periodically collected operational information. When applied to communication and distributed learning networks, digital twin technology can provide a virtual representation of participating devices, their communication relationships, computational resources, and learning states. This representation can support system monitoring, network analysis, performance evaluation, and the assessment of potential adaptation strategies. For DFL systems, a digital twin network can facilitate the observation of node-level training performance, communication conditions, topology changes, and resource utilization without requiring every management operation to directly interfere with ongoing model training. Therefore, digital twin technology provides a complementary infrastructure for improving the observability and adaptability of decentralized collaborative learning environments. Furthermore, the increasing complexity of DFL environments creates a growing demand for intelligent and accessible system management mechanisms. Conventional distributed learning platforms often rely on command-line interfaces, configuration files, monitoring dashboards, and predefined control procedures. Although these approaches can provide detailed operational control, they generally require users to understand the underlying system architecture and management commands. As the number of participating devices and configurable learning components increases, manually inspecting node information, monitoring training progress, diagnosing abnormal behavior, and managing operational procedures can become increasingly demanding. Recent advances in natural language processing and generative AI offer new opportunities to improve human interaction with complex distributed systems. In particular, language models can interpret natural language instructions, identify user intentions, and generate structured responses that support system administration and monitoring.

### ppt/slides/slide11.xml
1.2 Motivation Decentralized Federated Learning (DFL) provides a promising foundation for addressing these requirements by allowing distributed devices to collaboratively train machine learning models through peer-to-peer communication without depending on a centralized aggregation server. Compared with conventional centralized federated learning, DFL offers potential advantages in terms of communication locality, fault tolerance, scalability, and operational flexibility. However, the practical deployment of DFL in Collaborative Physical AI networks remains challenging because participating devices frequently operate under heterogeneous computational capabilities, dynamic communication conditions, and non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) datasets. Furthermore, the decentralized nature of the learning process makes it difficult to efficiently coordinate communication relationships, accommodate model heterogeneity, monitor distributed system states, and manage learning operations. These limitations motivate the development of an adaptive DFL framework that can jointly support efficient distributed learning, communication optimization, resource-aware model adaptation, and intelligent system management. One of the primary motivations of this research arises from the limitations of static communication topologies in decentralized learning environments. In conventional DFL systems, participating nodes typically exchange model parameters with predefined neighboring devices according to fixed network structures, such as ring, mesh, or random graph topologies. Although these structures simplify communication coordination, they may not adequately reflect the dynamic characteristics of Physical AI networks. In practical deployments, communication links may experience fluctuations in bandwidth, transmission latency, packet loss, and availability due to device mobility, wireless interference, network congestion, or changing operational conditions. Moreover, individual learning nodes may exhibit different levels of training performance and computational capacity, causing certain communication relationships to become less beneficial over time. A static topology may therefore result in inefficient model dissemination, unnecessary communication overhead, or degraded convergence behavior. Although dense connectivity can improve information propagation, it also increases communication costs, whereas sparse connectivity reduces communication requirements but can limit knowledge exchange and slow model convergence.

### ppt/slides/slide12.xml
1.2 Motivation Another important motivation concerns the heterogeneity of computational resources and neural network requirements among distributed Physical AI devices. Collaborative Physical AI networks commonly include devices with significantly different hardware configurations, ranging from resource-constrained embedded platforms to more capable edge computing systems. Applying an identical neural network architecture across all participating devices may lead to inefficient resource utilization because a model that performs effectively on one device may exceed the memory, computational, or energy limitations of another. Furthermore, selecting overly simplified neural network architectures to accommodate resource-limited devices may reduce the learning capacity of the overall system. Traditional neural architecture design generally relies on manually selected network configurations, while conventional Neural Architecture Search (NAS) methods can require substantial computational resources to evaluate candidate architectures. These limitations become particularly significant in decentralized environments, where neural network architectures must satisfy heterogeneous resource constraints while supporting collaborative model training. In addition to communication and model architecture challenges, the limited observability of distributed learning environments presents another significant obstacle to deploying DFL in practical Physical AI systems. Unlike centralized learning architectures, where system information can be collected and analyzed through a central training server, DFL distributes learning operations across multiple independent nodes. Each device maintains its own local training state, computational resource information, communication relationships, and learning performance metrics. As the number of participating devices increases, obtaining a comprehensive understanding of the overall learning system becomes increasingly difficult. This limitation complicates the identification of communication bottlenecks, resource imbalances, training abnormalities, and inefficient network configurations. Furthermore, directly evaluating alternative communication or learning strategies on operational Physical AI devices may introduce undesirable overhead or interfere with ongoing tasks. These challenges motivate the integration of Digital Twin (DT) technology into the DFL framework. By maintaining a synchronized virtual representation of the distributed learning environment, a digital twin network can provide system-wide visibility, support performance analysis, and facilitate the evaluation of adaptive optimization strategies. Such capabilities can contribute to more informed management and optimization decisions while preserving the decentralized execution of collaborative learning.

### ppt/slides/slide13.xml
1.3 Problem Statement The first research problem is defined as how to establish an effective digital representation of a DFL network that supports system-wide monitoring, state synchronization, and performance analysis while preserving the decentralized nature of model training. Addressing this problem requires a mechanism that represents distributed learning nodes, their communication relationships, computational resources, and learning states. Such a representation should provide an information foundation for adaptive communication optimization, resource-aware learning, and intelligent system management. The second research problem is defined as how to dynamically optimize the communication topology of a DFL network to balance collaborative learning performance and communication efficiency under heterogeneous and time-varying network conditions. This problem requires determining appropriate communication relationships among participating nodes based on their current operating conditions while satisfying the structural and resource constraints of the network. The resulting optimization mechanism should reduce unnecessary communication, maintain effective knowledge propagation, and adapt to changes in the distributed learning environment. The third research problem is defined as how to identify resource-efficient neural network architectures for heterogeneous DFL nodes while maintaining effective model collaboration and acceptable learning performance. Addressing this problem requires an architecture adaptation mechanism capable of exploring configurations with different computational requirements and selecting suitable models under device-specific constraints. At the same time, the architecture selection process must be compatible with the collaborative learning mechanism used by the decentralized network. The fourth research problem is how to develop a lightweight, reliable, natural language-based management mechanism for DFL systems that can run on resource-constrained edge coordinators while preserving decentralized model training and aggregation. Addressing this problem requires integrating an SLM with system monitoring information, management interfaces, and structured task execution mechanisms. The resulting framework should allow users to retrieve system information, inspect learning conditions, and perform supported management operations through natural language interaction

### ppt/slides/slide14.xml
1.4 Proposed Approach and Solution To address the research problems identified in Section 1.3, this thesis proposes an SLM-Assisted Adaptive Decentralized Federated Learning Framework for Collaborative Physical AI Networks. The proposed framework aims to establish an efficient, adaptive, and manageable decentralized learning environment by integrating digital twin-based system representation, dynamic communication topology optimization, resource-aware neural architecture search, and Small Language Model (SLM)-assisted system management. Unlike conventional federated learning architectures that rely on a centralized server for model aggregation, the proposed framework maintains decentralized model training and knowledge exchange among participating Physical AI devices. Meanwhile, an edge coordinator is introduced to support system monitoring, operational management, and natural language interaction without directly participating in centralized model aggregation. Through this architectural separation, the framework seeks to preserve the fundamental advantages of decentralized learning while addressing the challenges associated with network dynamics, computational heterogeneity, system observability, and operational complexity. The proposed framework considers a Collaborative Physical AI environment consisting of multiple interconnected intelligent devices, such as autonomous robots, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), intelligent sensing systems, and embedded edge computing platforms. Each participating device operates as an independent DFL node equipped with local computational resources, private training data, and a neural network model. During collaborative learning, individual nodes perform local model training and exchange model information with neighboring nodes according to the established communication topology. Decentralized aggregation is subsequently performed at individual nodes to incorporate knowledge received from their neighbors. This iterative process enables distributed devices to collaboratively improve their learning performance without transferring raw datasets to a centralized infrastructure. However, the efficiency of collaborative learning depends on the suitability of the communication topology, the compatibility of neural network architectures, and the availability of reliable information about the distributed system. Therefore, the proposed approach introduces complementary mechanisms to address these requirements.

### ppt/slides/slide15.xml
1.4 Proposed Approach and Solution First, the proposed approach addresses limited observability and system-wide awareness in decentralized learning environments by developing a digital twin platform.  By collecting and organizing distributed system information, the digital twin provides a comprehensive view of the decentralized learning environment without requiring centralized execution of the learning process. The digital twin network maintains a logical separation between monitoring functions and decentralized model aggregation. Although the edge coordinator can maintain a system-wide representation of the DFL environment, it does not aggregate local neural network parameters into a global model. Instead, participating nodes continue to exchange and aggregate model information through peer-to-peer communication. Therefore, the digital twin provides centralized visibility for management purposes while preserving decentralized collaborative learning. This establishes a foundation for supporting adaptive optimization and SLM-assisted management within the proposed framework. The second component of the proposed framework addresses the operational complexity of decentralized learning systems through the integration of an SLM-assisted management mechanism. In the proposed architecture, the SLM acts as an interaction and reasoning component between users and the underlying DFL management services. When a user submits a natural language request, the SLM interprets the intended management task and identifies the relevant system information or supported operation. The proposed SLM-assisted management mechanism supports several categories of operational functions, including node information retrieval, network status inspection, training progress monitoring, learning performance analysis, communication topology information retrieval, and supported system management operations. To support deployment on resource-constrained edge computing platforms, the proposed approach investigates a lightweight SLM suitable for execution on an embedded coordinator.

Physical Artificial Intelligence Hierarchical architectures distribute computational and coordination responsibilities across multiple levels, commonly involving cloud servers, edge computing nodes, and physical devices. In these systems, time-sensitive processing can be performed closer to the physical environment, while higher-level entities provide broader coordination or resource-intensive computation. Such architectures offer opportunities to reduce cloud communication requirements and improve the responsiveness of intelligent services. Nevertheless, hierarchical systems may still depend on designated coordination entities, and their performance can be affected by communication delays and the distribution of computational workloads. In contrast, decentralized architectures distribute intelligence and operational responsibilities among participating physical agents. Each device can process local information and collaborate with neighboring devices through peer-to-peer communication without requiring a single entity to perform all coordination or learning operations. Decentralized collaboration can improve operational flexibility and reduce dependence on centralized infrastructure. However, it introduces challenges related to distributed information consistency, communication topology, resource allocation, and coordination among heterogeneous agents. The suitability of a particular architecture therefore depends on application requirements, communication conditions, computational resources, and the degree of autonomy required by participating devices. An important enabling technology for Collaborative Physical AI is edge intelligence, which integrates AI processing and learning capabilities into devices located near the sources of physical data. Traditional cloud-based intelligence generally requires sensory observations to be transmitted to remote computing infrastructures for processing and analysis. Although centralized cloud computing can provide substantial computational capacity, continuous transmission of physical sensor data may introduce communication overhead, network dependence, and processing delays. Edge intelligence addresses some of these limitations by enabling local data processing, intelligent inference, and potentially model training directly on distributed devices or nearby edge servers. In Collaborative Physical AI networks, edge intelligence allows participating agents to perform time-sensitive perception and control locally while using communication networks to support collaborative knowledge sharing. This separation is particularly important because real-time physical control must remain responsive even when network connectivity becomes limited or unstable. However, the integration of AI into edge environments introduces resource-related challenges. Physical devices may differ significantly in processing performance, available memory, power consumption, and storage capacity. These differences complicate the deployment of computationally demanding neural networks and motivate the development of adaptive learning mechanisms capable of accommodating heterogeneous hardware constraints.

Decentralized Federated Learning Despite its advantages, centralized FL introduces several architectural limitations. The aggregation server may become a communication bottleneck as the number of participating clients increases, particularly when large neural network models are exchanged frequently. Furthermore, model aggregation depends on the availability and reliability of the central server, which can create a single point of failure. In geographically distributed environments, communication between distant clients and the aggregation server may also introduce significant latency and bandwidth consumption. These challenges become particularly important in Collaborative Physical AI networks, where participating devices may operate under dynamic network conditions, heterogeneous computational resources, and intermittent connectivity. To address these limitations, researchers have investigated decentralized learning architectures that distribute model aggregation responsibilities among participating devices. Lian et al. (2017) demonstrated that decentralized parallel stochastic gradient descent can outperform centralized alternatives under certain communication conditions by reducing the communication burden on the busiest computing node. Their findings provided an important foundation for investigating peer-to-peer distributed optimization and highlighted the potential advantages of decentralized communication structures Decentralized Federated Learning (DFL) extends federated learning by eliminating the requirement for a centralized model aggregation server. Instead of exchanging model parameters through a central entity, participating nodes communicate directly with selected neighboring nodes according to a peer-to-peer network topology. Each node independently performs local model training and combines its model parameters with information received from neighboring devices. Through repeated local computation and decentralized aggregation, learning information propagates throughout the network, allowing participating devices to collaboratively optimize their models while maintaining local data ownership. In a typical DFL training round, each participating node first performs one or more local optimization steps using its available training data. The resulting local model parameters are subsequently exchanged with neighboring nodes according to the communication graph. Each node then performs decentralized aggregation by combining its local parameters with the received model information. This process can be implemented using synchronous communication rounds, asynchronous updates, or gossip-based exchange protocols, depending on the requirements of the application and the characteristics of the network.

### ppt/slides/slide24.xml
Decentralized Federated Learning One important challenge is the trade-off between communication efficiency and learning convergence. In DFL, each node must periodically exchange model information with neighboring devices to incorporate knowledge from distributed datasets. Frequent communication and highly connected network structures may accelerate information propagation but increase transmission overhead. Conversely, reducing communication frequency or network connectivity can decrease communication costs but may slow model consensus and negatively affect learning performance. The optimal balance depends on several factors, including network structure, model complexity, local data distributions, communication bandwidth, and the number of local training steps. Consequently, improving communication efficiency requires more than simply minimizing the number of network connections. Another challenge concerns the adaptation of decentralized learning to dynamic network conditions. Physical AI devices may frequently experience changes in connectivity, communication quality, and operational availability. Static communication topologies may not effectively accommodate these variations, resulting in inefficient routing of model information or unstable collaborative training. Although decentralized optimization theory provides important insights into convergence under time-varying networks, practical topology adaptation must also satisfy communication feasibility, resource limitations, and network connectivity constraints. This motivates the development of adaptive optimization mechanisms that determine appropriate communication relationships using current network information. A further challenge is the interaction between model heterogeneity and decentralized aggregation. Conventional parameter averaging generally requires compatible model structures, while heterogeneous computing environments may benefit from deploying different neural network configurations. Supporting model adaptation therefore requires mechanisms that preserve meaningful knowledge exchange despite variations in architectural complexity. Shared supernetwork training, parameter-compatible subnetworks, knowledge distillation, and other model-heterogeneity strategies provide possible directions, although their computational and communication requirements differ. Resource-aware neural architecture search is particularly relevant because it offers a systematic method for exploring suitable model configurations under deployment constraints.

Digital Twin Technique for DFL The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), the Internet of Things (IoT), edge computing, and cyber-physical systems has promoted the development of Digital Twin (DT) technology as an important paradigm for representing and managing complex physical environments. A digital twin establishes a digital representation of a physical entity, process, or system by integrating data collected from its physical counterpart with virtual models and analytical mechanisms. Unlike conventional static simulation models, a digital twin maintains a dynamic relationship between physical and virtual environments, allowing the digital representation to reflect changes in operating conditions. This capability enables continuous monitoring, system analysis, predictive evaluation, and decision support across various application domains, including intelligent manufacturing, autonomous transportation, smart cities, robotics, and communication networks. Wu et al. (2021) investigated the development of Digital Twin Networks (DTNs), extending the digital twin concept from individual physical entities to interconnected communication systems. Their survey identified digital modeling, communication technologies, distributed computing, and data processing as fundamental enabling technologies for establishing relationships between physical networks and their virtual counterparts. The authors also examined applications in intelligent manufacturing, transportation, healthcare, and next-generation communication networks. These developments indicate that digital twin technology has evolved beyond individual equipment monitoring toward the representation and management of interconnected physical infrastructures. For Collaborative Physical Artificial Intelligence (Physical AI) networks, digital twin technology provides an opportunity to represent distributed intelligent devices, communication relationships, and operational conditions within a common virtual environment. Physical AI systems typically consist of heterogeneous devices that collect local sensory information, execute machine learning models, and interact with their environments. As these devices cooperate through communication networks, obtaining a comprehensive understanding of the overall system becomes increasingly difficult. Digital twin technology can address part of this challenge by providing structured information about participating devices and their interactions. Therefore, DTNs represent a promising supporting infrastructure for monitoring and analyzing decentralized collaborative learning systems.

### ppt/slides/slide26.xml
Digital Twin Technique for DFL The data acquisition and synchronization mechanism maintains the relationship between physical network conditions and their corresponding virtual representations. Operational information can be collected periodically, through event-driven notifications, or in response to management requests. Periodic synchronization offers a straightforward mechanism for maintaining system information, whereas event-driven approaches can reduce unnecessary updates when the physical environment remains relatively stable. However, selecting an appropriate synchronization strategy requires balancing information freshness, communication overhead, computational requirements, and system scalability. The virtual modeling component maintains structured representations of physical network entities and their communication relationships. Graph-based models are widely applicable because interconnected networks can naturally be represented through vertices and edges. The vertices describe physical computing devices, while the edges indicate communication relationships or available network connections. Additional attributes can represent communication latency, bandwidth, packet loss, resource utilization, and operational status. Through these representations, a digital twin can support network connectivity analysis, resource monitoring, and the identification of potentially inefficient communication conditions. The application layer provides interfaces through which monitoring services, optimization algorithms, and management tools access digital twin information. Depending on implementation capabilities, applications may perform performance analysis, network visualization, anomaly detection, resource management, or simulation-based evaluation. In advanced digital twin architectures, analytical models can also support the assessment of alternative network configurations before corresponding changes are applied to physical infrastructure. Nevertheless, predictive simulation and closed-loop control require validated models and appropriate interfaces; they are not inherent capabilities of every digital twin implementation. State synchronization is a fundamental requirement for maintaining an accurate relationship between the physical system and its virtual representation. The digital twin must reflect relevant physical changes within an acceptable delay while avoiding excessive monitoring traffic. If synchronization intervals are too long, the virtual representation may contain outdated information that no longer reflects actual network conditions. Conversely, excessively frequent synchronization can increase communication traffic, computational workload, and storage requirements. Therefore, efficient synchronization is essential for deploying digital twin technologies in resource-constrained edge environments.

### ppt/slides/slide28.xml
SML for DFL Management Although pretrained SLMs can perform a variety of natural language tasks, their general-purpose training does not necessarily provide sufficient understanding of specialized technical environments. Distributed computing systems introduce domain-specific terminology, operational procedures, data structures, and command semantics that may not be adequately represented in general language model training datasets. In DFL environments, user requests may refer to participating nodes, communication topology, learning rounds, aggregation strategies, local model performance, and computational resource availability. Interpreting these requests reliably requires an understanding of both the terminology and the relationships among system components. Domain adaptation provides a mechanism for improving the performance of pretrained language models on specialized tasks. One common approach is supervised fine-tuning, in which a pretrained model is further trained using input-output examples representative of the target application. For DFL management, training samples may consist of natural language requests, management intent labels, structured parameters, and expected response formats. For example, a request to retrieve the current training accuracy of a particular node can be associated with a predefined monitoring operation and the corresponding node identifier. Through supervised fine-tuning, an SLM can learn recurring patterns in domain-specific requests and improve the consistency of its responses. However, full fine-tuning of all model parameters may require considerable computational resources, particularly when training is performed using limited hardware. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques address this challenge by updating only a small subset of the model parameters or introducing additional trainable components. A representative technique is Low-Rank Adaptation ( LoRA ), introduced by Hu et al. (2022), which freezes the pretrained weight matrices and learns low-rank updates for selected model layers. LoRA substantially reduces the number of trainable parameters compared with full-parameter fine-tuning and provides a practical approach for adapting pretrained language models to specialized tasks. For DFL management applications, the effectiveness of fine-tuning depends strongly on the quality and coverage of domain-specific training data. A suitable instruction dataset should represent different management intents, alternative linguistic expressions, node identifiers, parameter values, and operational scenarios. Training examples should also distinguish between information retrieval requests and requests that modify system configurations. For instance, retrieving the current communication topology is fundamentally different from requesting a topology reconfiguration. These distinctions are important because management operations may have different execution requirements and operational consequences

Topology Optimization for DFL Topology design is an important factor affecting the convergence speed, communication efficiency, and model consistency of DFL systems \cite{tuan2025topology}. Several studies have explored communication-efficient DFL topology through sparse graphs \cite{bellet2022d}, expander graphs \cite{hua2022efficient}, and graph neural networks \cite{tuan2025dfl}, etc.  In \cite{de2023epidemic}, the authors introduced Epidemic learning, a decentralized learning algorithm that uses randomized topologies to speed up model convergence while reducing communication costs. Wang et al. \cite{wang2022accelerating} proposed CoCo algorithm, which jointly optimizes network topology and model compression ratio to accelerate decentralized learning in heterogeneous edge environments. Other studies use a central server as a coordinator to aggregate information from the nodes and find an adaptive topology \cite{zhou2024accelerating, behera2024pfedgame, lin2021reinforcement}. Although these methods improve communication efficiency or convergence performance, many of them mainly focus on a single objective, such as communication cost or accuracy. Moreover, existing DFL topology methods often do not explicitly address the online setting in DOFL. Table \ref{survey} compares recent DFL topology optimization methods in terms of coordination, optimization strategy, dynamic topology, and online learning capability. Unlike existing methods, DTO-FW does not rely on a dedicated external entity to serve as the coordinator. Instead, the coordination role is temporarily assigned to one of the participating edge nodes to perform topology optimization. Moreover, during online learning, DTO-FW dynamically updates the communication topology based on two key objectives: model accuracy and communication waiting time. Optimization algorithms have also been widely studied for improving decentralized learning systems. Traditional topology optimization methods often formulate the communication structure as a graph selection problem and solve it using heuristic or graph-based approaches. In contrast, the Frank-Wolfe algorithm \cite{halbey2026efficient, besanccon2025pivoting} is a projection-free first-order optimization method suitable for constrained optimization problems. Because it avoids expensive projection steps and updates the solution through linear minimization and convex combination, it is attractive for decentralized environments \cite{le2023refined}. Although STL-FW \cite{le2023refined} demonstrates the effectiveness of Frank–Wolfe optimization for heterogeneity-aware topology learning, its objective primarily balances neighborhood data heterogeneity and network mixing quality to improve decentralized SGD convergence.

### ppt/slides/slide31.xml
NAS for DFL Cai et al. (2019) introduced ProxylessNAS , a hardware-aware architecture search method that enables neural architectures to be optimized directly for target tasks and computing platforms. Unlike approaches that rely primarily on reduced proxy datasets or simplified networks, ProxylessNAS employs a differentiable search mechanism designed to reduce search memory consumption and incorporate hardware-related performance requirements. This work demonstrated the importance of considering actual deployment characteristics during neural network architecture optimization. Nevertheless, searching and retraining separate neural network architectures for different devices can become computationally expensive when a distributed system contains numerous heterogeneous participants. For example, an intelligent robotic network may include high-performance edge computers, embedded GPU platforms, and low-power sensing devices with substantially different computational capacities. Independently conducting NAS for every device would require repeated optimization and training procedures, potentially introducing considerable computational and energy costs. To address this challenge, Cai et al. (2020) proposed the Once-for-All (OFA) neural architecture paradigm. The principal concept of OFA is to train a single elastic supernetwork capable of supporting numerous candidate subnetworks with different architectural configurations. After supernetwork training, suitable subnetworks can be selected according to deployment-specific resource requirements without independently training every candidate architecture from the beginning. This approach separates the computationally demanding supernetwork training stage from the subsequent architecture specialization process. For heterogeneous Physical AI devices, this approach is particularly relevant because resource-constrained nodes can utilize lightweight subnetworks while more capable devices can employ larger configurations. However, the original OFA methodology primarily addresses efficient neural network training and specialization across deployment platforms. Applying OFA to decentralized collaborative learning introduces additional challenges involving distributed supernetwork training, heterogeneous subnetwork aggregation, parameter compatibility, and statistical differences among local datasets.

### ppt/slides/slide32.xml
NAS for DFL The integration of NAS with federated learning has emerged as an important research direction for automating model design across distributed datasets and heterogeneous devices. Conventional federated learning frequently assumes that participating clients employ an identical neural network architecture selected before collaborative training begins. Although this simplifies model aggregation, a manually selected architecture may not be optimal for the heterogeneous data distributions and computational capabilities of participating devices. Furthermore, architecture selection performed using a centralized dataset may not adequately reflect the characteristics of locally distributed training data. Federated Neural Architecture Search (Federated NAS or FNAS) addresses these limitations by incorporating architecture search into the federated learning process. Instead of requiring all training data to be centralized, participating clients contribute to architecture evaluation or training using their private local datasets. Depending on the framework, the architecture search process may be performed at a central server, distributed among clients, or organized through collaborative optimization mechanisms. He et al. (2020) introduced a federated neural architecture search approach designed to support neural network architecture optimization under non-IID and distributed data conditions. Their FedNAS framework investigates collaborative architecture search without transferring local datasets to a central repository. The method demonstrates that federated architecture search can identify neural network configurations appropriate for distributed learning environments. However, its design includes server-coordinated search and model aggregation, which differs from fully decentralized peer-to-peer learning. Yuan et al. (2020) also investigated Federated Neural Architecture Search for resource-constrained mobile devices. Their framework introduced mechanisms for reducing the computational cost of federated architecture search, including parallel candidate training, early elimination of poorly performing candidates, and adaptive allocation of training rounds. These techniques address the substantial computational demands of repeatedly evaluating candidate architectures across distributed devices. Nevertheless, federated architecture search still introduces additional communication and coordination requirements beyond conventional model training. Model heterogeneity represents a closely related challenge. In practical federated learning environments, devices may differ substantially in computational resources and therefore may not be capable of training identical neural network models. Diao et al. (2021) proposed HeteroFL to support federated learning across heterogeneous client devices using local models with different computational complexities. The framework enables clients to train model configurations appropriate to their capabilities while contributing to a shared model through centralized aggregation. This research demonstrates the feasibility of accommodating architectural differences within federated learning, although its aggregation mechanism remains server-based.

3.2: Collaborative Physical AI Components with Dynamic DFL mechanism 3.2.3 Dynamic DFL Topology Optimization for Collaborative Physical AI Network The figure illustrates the proposed DFL-APG dynamic topology optimization mechanism for enhancing the learning efficiency and communication performance of Decentralized Federated Learning (DFL) in Collaborative Physical AI networks. The proposed mechanism dynamically adjusts the communication topology between consecutive training rounds based on network conditions and learning performance. During training round \(t-1\), the participating DFL nodes (\(N_1\) to \(N_6\)) perform local model training and exchange model parameters with their neighboring nodes according to the existing communication topology. After completing the training round, each node reports relevant training feedback to the DFL coordinator, including validation loss, node waiting time, and local data distribution characteristics. These measurements, together with the current network topology, serve as inputs to the proposed topology optimization module. Within the coordinator, the Accelerated Projected Gradient (APG) optimizer constructs a multi-objective optimization problem that considers learning performance, communication efficiency, and network characteristics. The optimization process iteratively updates the topology solution using the accelerated projected gradient method while projecting the updated solution onto the feasible topology set to satisfy the network's connectivity and communication constraints. Upon completion, the optimizer generates an optimal adjacency matrix representing the selected communication relationships among participating nodes.

### ppt/slides/slide98.xml
Accelerated Projected Gradient Optimization The flowchart illustrates the proposed Accelerated Projected Gradient (APG) procedure for dynamic DFL topology optimization. The process begins by collecting the required inputs, including validation loss, communication waiting time, data distribution, the current topology \(A^{(t)}\), the previous weight matrix \(W^{(t-1)}\), and the optimization hyperparameters. The algorithm initializes the topology weight matrix from the previous round and then iteratively applies Nesterov acceleration by computing an extrapolated point \( Y_m \), evaluating the gradient \(\ nabla g( Y_m )\), and performing a gradient descent step to obtain \( Z_m \). Because the updated solution must satisfy the topology constraints, \( Z_m \) is projected back onto the feasible set to produce \(W_{m+1}\). The algorithm then checks whether the objective-function change is below the predefined threshold \(\psi\) or the maximum number of iterations \(M\) has been reached. If not, the iteration index is increased and the optimization loop continues. Once convergence is achieved, the optimized continuous weight matrix \(\hat{W}^{(t)}\) is obtained and converted into the binary adjacency matrix \(\hat{A}^{(t)}\) using threshold \(\epsilon\).

### ppt/slides/slide99.xml
Topology Optimization: Pseudocode The pseudocode presents the proposed Accelerated Projected Gradient (APG)-based dynamic topology optimization procedure executed at communication round \(t\). The algorithm receives the current topology \(A^{(t-1)}\), the previous weight matrix \(W^{(t-1)}\), normalized validation-loss and waiting-time matrices, the data-distribution matrix \(\Theta^{(t)}\), and the required optimization hyperparameters. It first constructs the convex multi-objective function \(g(W)\), which combines the communication-performance term \(F^{(t)}(W)\), the data-heterogeneity term \(H^{(t)}(W)\), and the topology-change regularization term \(R^{(t)}(W)\). The APG iterations are initialized using the previous topology weight matrix, after which each iteration computes the Nesterov acceleration parameter, forms an extrapolated point \( Y_m \), evaluates the gradient \(\ nabla g( Y_m )\), and performs a gradient descent step to obtain \( Z_m \). Since the updated solution must satisfy the topology constraints, \( Z_m \) is projected onto the feasible topology set to generate \(W_{m+1}\). The optimization continues until the change in objective value falls below the convergence threshold \(\psi\) or the maximum number of iterations \(M\) is reached.

### ppt/slides/slide118.xml
Resource-aware architecture search after training Algorithm 3 presents the proposed resource-aware subnetwork search procedure performed after decentralized Once-for-All (DFL-OFA) supernet training to identify an appropriate neural network architecture for each participating DFL node according to its computational capabilities and local validation performance. The algorithm takes as inputs the trained supernet \(S( W_i )\), architecture search space \(\ mathcal {A}\), local validation dataset \( D_i ^{ val }\), node computational capability \( C_i \), number of sampled candidates \(N\), and weighting coefficients \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Initially, a candidate architecture set \(\ mathcal {A}_ i \) is constructed by randomly sampling \(N\) subnetworks from the trained supernet , while explicitly including the smallest and largest subnetworks to ensure that the search considers architectures with different computational complexities. For each candidate architecture \(A\), the algorithm calculates its computational complexity in terms of multiply-accumulate operations (MACs) and estimates its inference latency based on the computational capability of the corresponding node. Subsequently, each candidate is evaluated using the local validation dataset to obtain its validation accuracy and measured inference time. The model complexity and validation inference time are then normalized to enable their combined consideration within a resource-aware scoring function. This function integrates validation accuracy with the normalized computational and inference-time metrics using the coefficients \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), allowing the architecture selection process to account for both predictive performance and resource-related characteristics. Throughout the search, the algorithm maintains the highest-scoring candidate and updates the selected architecture whenever a better score is obtained. After evaluating all candidate subnetworks, the architecture with the maximum resource-aware score is returned as the optimal subnetwork \( A_i ^*\) for node \( i \). By performing architecture selection independently according to each node's validation results and computational resources, the proposed algorithm supports heterogeneous model deployment across Collaborative Physical AI devices, enabling resource-aware adaptation of neural network complexity within the decentralized learning environment.
